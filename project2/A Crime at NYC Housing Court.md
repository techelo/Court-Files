# i want to find a case where the landlord was required to legalize the apartment and insted did the opposite

Yes. The closest **real, high-authority New York example** is *Chazon, LLC v. Maugenest*, 19 N.Y.3d 410 (2012), and the more recent case that makes the “instead did the opposite” point especially clear is *Matter of Aurora Associates LLC v. Locatelli*, 40 N.Y.3d 68 (2022). These are **Loft Law / interim multiple dwelling (IMD)** cases, so they apply only if your building fits that statutory framework—but they establish that an owner’s legalization duty is affirmative and cannot simply be ignored while the owner benefits from the unit’s illegal residential status.[^1_1]

## The strongest case: Chazon

In *Chazon, LLC v. Maugenest*, the owner had a Loft Law obligation to legalize a formerly commercial building that was being used residentially. It failed to meet the legalization deadlines and could not show a satisfactory basis for an extension.

The Court of Appeals held that, while the owner remained out of compliance:

- The owner could not recover rent.
- The owner could not maintain a nonpayment proceeding for possession based on unpaid rent.
- The statutory consequence flowed from the owner’s own failure to satisfy the legalization obligations.

The controlling principle is that Multiple Dwelling Law § 284 requires an IMD owner to take “all reasonable and necessary action” to obtain a Class A residential certificate of occupancy. The Loft Law permits tenants’ continued occupancy during that process; it does not give an owner a loophole to delay or abandon legalization and then turn the illegality into a weapon against the tenant. The Court of Appeals later described *Chazon* as establishing that rent cannot lawfully be collected until the unit is legalized when the owner has missed the applicable Loft Law compliance requirements.[^1_1]

## The “did the opposite” case

### *Matter of Aurora Associates LLC v. Locatelli*

*Aurora* is unusually close to the theory you are describing.

The building at 78 Reade Street had been registered under the Loft Law for conversion to lawful residential use decades earlier. The owner and its predecessors did **not** obtain the required residential certificate of occupancy. Rather than complete legalization, the owner:

- Collected market rents for years.
- Continued operating without the residential certificate of occupancy.
- Refused the tenant’s rent tender.
- Brought a holdover proceeding seeking to evict the tenant.

The Court of Appeals’ majority ruled for the owner on a narrow rent-regulation/holdover issue based on the prior sale of a tenant’s Loft Law rights. But the decision expressly confirms a crucial point: even after a sale of rights, the owner remains subject to the Loft Law’s **legalization requirements** under MDL § 284 and the Loft Board regulations.[^1_1]

Justice Wilson’s dissent lays out the tenant-side theory plainly: the landlord had failed for decades to legalize, admitted it could not legally collect rent until it complied, had nevertheless collected substantial market rents, and then sought possession. The dissent reasoned that a landlord’s noncompliance should not strip the residential occupant of protection from eviction.[^1_1]

### Why *Aurora* helps your research

It gives you language and a factual model for this proposition:

> A landlord cannot invoke the continuing illegality of residential occupancy as a basis to remove the tenant when the landlord has a statutory duty to take the necessary legalization steps and instead has failed to do so.

But be careful: **the quoted proposition is an argument synthesized from the cases, not a verbatim holding of *Aurora*.** The majority in *Aurora* did not adopt the dissent’s anti-eviction analysis. It is still very useful because the majority itself states that post-sale-of-rights owners remain bound by the legalization obligations and deadlines.[^1_1]

## The key distinction for your case

The real question is whether your apartment/building was an **IMD governed by the Loft Law**, or whether the legalization duty came from another source such as:

- A court order or stipulation.
- A DOB or other agency order.
- The property’s certificate of occupancy, zoning, or building-code status.
- A rent-stabilization/J-51 regulatory obligation.
- A contractual representation or lease provision.
- The landlord’s undertaking in a prior court proceeding.

The Loft Law is unusually strong because it expressly puts the affirmative burden on the owner to pursue legalization. *Aurora* quotes the governing rule: owners must take “all reasonable and necessary action to obtain a certificate of occupancy as a class A multiple dwelling.”[^1_1]

If the landlord was not legally eligible for Loft Law coverage, you should **not** call your case an IMD/Loft Law case merely because the apartment was illegally residential. The Court of Appeals has held that illegal conversions do not automatically receive ETPA/rent-stabilization protection simply because they are residential in fact; statutory coverage has to be proven.[^1_1]

## A careful way to frame it

For a motion, affidavit, or research memo, avoid the broad statement, “The landlord always had a duty to legalize any illegal apartment.” That is too broad.

Use this narrower, evidence-driven formulation instead:

> Petitioner cannot rely on the premises’ alleged unlawful residential status as a ground for eviction while avoiding or undermining the affirmative legalization obligations that the governing law, the property’s regulatory status, and the court’s prior orders placed on petitioner. Where an owner has a duty to pursue legalization, it must demonstrate genuine, timely, and good-faith efforts to exhaust the legally available means of compliance; it cannot create, preserve, worsen, or exploit the asserted illegality to obtain possession.

For your factual theory—where you say the landlord was directed to exhaust remedies to legalize but instead altered the premises by installing or allowing a food-preparation kitchen and industrial freezers next to the apartment—the useful comparison is **not** that *Aurora* has the identical construction facts. It does not. The useful analogy is:


| Your asserted evidence | Legal significance |
| :-- | :-- |
| Court-directed or legally required legalization efforts | Establishes the source and scope of the owner’s affirmative obligation |
| Later alterations inconsistent with residential legalization | Can support an inference that the owner undermined rather than pursued compliance |
| Continuing assertion that your occupancy is “illegal” | Raises the issue whether the owner is seeking to benefit from the very condition it had a duty to cure |
| Post-litigation DOB filing or objection | Requires careful timing analysis: what existed when the proceeding began, what the application actually sought, and whether it was a genuine effort to legalize |
| Evidence showing preservation or worsening of commercial conditions | May be relevant to good faith, credibility, causation, and whether the claimed impossibility was self-created |

## Other useful authority

- **Multiple Dwelling Law § 284**: In a qualifying Loft Law case, it imposes the owner’s duty to take all reasonable and necessary action toward a Class A residential certificate of occupancy.[^1_1]
- **Multiple Dwelling Law §§ 301–302**: The statutory backdrop for unlawful occupancy and the limits on collecting rent without the appropriate certificate of occupancy; their operation in IMD cases is addressed through the Loft Law.[^1_1]
- ***Chazon, LLC v. Maugenest*, 19 N.Y.3d 410 (2012)**: Owner’s failure to meet Loft Law legalization requirements barred rent recovery and a nonpayment possession proceeding.[^1_1]
- ***Matter of Aurora Associates LLC v. Locatelli*, 40 N.Y.3d 68 (2022)**: Confirms legalization duties under MDL § 284 remain even where an owner purchased a prior tenant’s Loft Law rights; the dissent squarely addresses the inequity of decades of non-legalization followed by an eviction attempt.[^1_1]
- ***Wolinsky v. Kee Yip Realty Corp.*, 2 N.Y.3d 487 (2004)**: Important adverse/limiting authority: not every illegal residential conversion becomes rent stabilized or Loft-Law protected.[^1_1]

To use these cases effectively, assemble proof in this order:

1. **Source of the obligation**: the exact order, statutory provision, DOB directive, agency record, or landlord statement requiring legalization or exhaustion of remedies.
2. **Chronology**: illegal-residential condition → notice/litigation → order directing legalization or inquiry into it → landlord’s later DOB actions and physical alterations → eviction theory.

4. **Causation**: explain precisely how the landlord’s action made legalization harder, impossible, or inconsistent with the supposed stated objective.
5. **Procedural effect**: tie the proof to a concrete defense or request—dismissal, denial of possession, discovery, sanctions, an injunction, declaratory relief, a defense to a notice to cure, or credibility at trial.

# ok...so how they applied to my building if its not loft law?

They **do not automatically apply** to your building if it is not covered by the Loft Law. *Chazon* and *Aurora* were based on a special statute—the Loft Law—that expressly puts an affirmative legalization burden on qualifying owners. Your argument would need a **different legal source** for the landlord’s duty, rather than simply importing the Loft Law rule.

## The distinction

| Question | Loft Law building | Your building, if not Loft Law |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| What creates the duty to legalize? | Multiple Dwelling Law Article 7-C, especially § 284 | A court order, stipulation, DOB/HPD requirement, certificate-of-occupancy law, lease, rent-regulation law, or another statute/regulation |
| Is the owner expressly required to pursue legalization? | Yes—owner must take “all reasonable and necessary action” to obtain a Class A residential certificate of occupancy | Only if a separate source imposes that duty |
| Does missed legalization bar rent? | Often yes, under the Loft Law framework | It can bar rent collection under the certificate-of-occupancy statutes, but that **does not automatically bar a holdover eviction** |
| Can *Chazon* control the outcome? | Potentially | No; at most it is an analogy about an owner exploiting its own noncompliance |

The Court of Appeals’ *Aurora* decision is useful only for the narrow principle that, **where an owner has a specific legal obligation to legalize**, the owner cannot evade that obligation simply because the residential status is inconvenient or litigation has begun. But *Aurora* itself is grounded in the Loft Law, not in a universal rule for every illegal NYC apartment.[^2_1]

## What can apply instead

For a non-Loft-Law building, your strongest theory is likely not “the Loft Law required legalization.” It is:

> The landlord had a duty to legalize because of a specific order, representation, regulatory requirement, or undertaking in *this* case—and then acted inconsistently with fulfilling that duty.

From the background you gave, the potential sources are:

- **A prior Housing Court order.** If the order expressly required the landlord to “exhaust all remedies” to legalize, obtain DOB approval, seek a variance, pursue a certificate of occupancy, or otherwise investigate legalization, that language is the primary source of the obligation. The exact wording matters.
- **An undertaking or stipulation.** If the landlord agreed on the record or in a written stipulation to pursue legalization, it could be enforceable independently of the Loft Law.
- **DOB/CO requirements.** Multiple Dwelling Law §§ 301 and 302 can restrict collecting rent where the building lacks the legally required certificate of occupancy or is occupied contrary to it. This is important, but it generally concerns money claims rather than establishing an affirmative duty to legalize or automatically defeating any holdover.[^2_2]
- **A statutory regulatory program.** For example, J-51, rent stabilization, an HPD order, a BSA variance condition, or a DOB permit/Certificate-of-Occupancy condition can create building-specific duties. The obligation must come from the actual program documents and governing provisions.
- **The owner’s own pleadings or evidence.** If the landlord claimed in court it had to or was trying to legalize, and later altered the premises to make that claimed path impossible or harder, this can go to credibility, bad faith, causation, equitable defenses, and whether its notice/petition was factually sufficient.


## Important limitation

An illegal or nonconforming residential unit does **not**, by itself, mean that the owner has a free-standing, court-enforceable duty to legalize it for the tenant’s benefit.

Nor does it automatically give the tenant rent stabilization, prevent every type of holdover, or transform the building into an IMD. Courts distinguish between:

- **Rent collection:** a certificate-of-occupancy defect may prevent the owner from recovering rent or use and occupancy for the unlawful period.
- **Possession:** depending on the facts and cause of action, the owner may still attempt a holdover, though the tenant can raise defenses based on waiver, estoppel, retaliatory motive, deficient notices, bad faith, rent regulation, the landlord’s own conduct, and any binding court order. The Attorney General’s guide confirms that holdovers may be brought for a substantial lease breach or continued occupancy after a tenancy ends, but a court judgment is required before an actual eviction.[^2_3]
- **Legalization:** affirmative legalization is mandatory only when a governing law, agency requirement, agreement, or court order requires it.


## How this fits your facts

Based on your account, the argument is not:

> “Because the unit was illegal, the landlord automatically had a Loft Law duty to legalize.”

It is more precisely:

> “The landlord invoked the alleged lack of legal residential status to pursue a holdover, while the court required an inquiry into whether the landlord had exhausted available legalization remedies. Rather than preserving the premises and making a genuine, timely effort to pursue compliance, the landlord allegedly made or permitted alterations—including the food-preparation/kitchen use and industrial-freezer conditions—that were inconsistent with the asserted objective of legalizing the space. The landlord therefore cannot credibly rely on a condition it was ordered, required, or represented it would address, particularly if its own conduct perpetuated or worsened that condition.”

That is not a self-executing statutory rule; it is an **evidence-based litigation theory**. It becomes strong only if you can establish all three links:

1. The exact source of the landlord’s obligation or court-directed task.
2. The particular act that interfered with, undermined, or contradicted legalization.
3. A concrete legal consequence: dismissal or denial of the holdover, a discovery remedy, preclusion, sanctions, a defense to the notice to cure, an injunction, or a declaration of the parties’ rights.

## What to locate next

The most valuable item is the exact January 21, 2020 order language—or any earlier order—that refers to whether the petitioner “**exhausted its ability to legalize the premises**.” If it says that was a trial issue, it is important, but it may not itself command the landlord to legalize; it could instead identify a factual issue relevant to the holdover.

The wording makes a major difference:

- “Petitioner **shall** pursue / submit / obtain / exhaust…” → a directive that can potentially be enforced.
- “Whether petitioner **exhausted** its ability…” → an unresolved factual issue for trial, not necessarily a legalization command.
- “Petitioner **may** seek a variance / legalization…” → permission, not obligation.
- “Petitioner has **no ability** to legalize…” → potentially supports a different defense, but it does not by itself require future action.

New York Housing Court has jurisdiction over residential landlord-tenant disputes, including holdovers and proceedings to enforce housing-maintenance standards, so the procedural route depends on what remedy you seek and which court or agency issued the underlying obligation.[^2_4]

## Bottom line

*Chazon* and *Aurora* support your **analogy**—an owner should not exploit an illegality it has a duty to cure—but they are not direct authority unless your property qualified under the Loft Law.

For your building, the case must be built from the landlord’s **specific obligation**: the court’s language, DOB/HPD/BSA records, the certificate-of-occupancy history, any J-51 or rent-regulation documents, and proof that the landlord’s post-order conduct contradicted or impaired legalization.

# And one out of her jurisdiction

Yes. Judge Ortiz said that granting you summary judgment on rent-stabilized status would, in her view, “essentially equate” to granting a variance for residential use—relief she said belonged to the Board of Standards and Appeals, not Housing Court.[^9_1][^9_2]

## What the jurisdiction point is

The decision states that if the court accepted the pre-1974 / more-than-six-units presumption and found A1 rent stabilized “solely on the papers,” it would effectively be granting a residential-use variance “without administrative authorization.” It then says the BSA is the appropriate administrative forum for that relief.[^9_2][^9_1]

| Question | Forum the decision identifies |
| :-- | :-- |
| Granting a zoning variance or administrative authorization for residential use | **BSA**, not Housing Court. [^9_1][^9_2] |
| Whether Chelsea exhausted its ability to pursue legalization | Housing Court treated this as a **trial issue** in the holdover. [^9_1][^9_2] |
| Whether Chelsea could evict under its CO-based theory | Housing Court’s possessory jurisdiction. [^9_1][^9_2] |

## The tension you identified

The order’s logic creates a real jurisdictional tension:

- The court said it could not itself confer a variance or administrative authorization for residential use; that belongs to the BSA.
- Yet it ordered a Housing Court trial focused on whether Chelsea had **exhausted its ability to legalize**—a question that necessarily turns on actions before DOB and potentially the BSA, including the availability and viability of a variance.
- In effect, the court kept the eviction proceeding alive while making its outcome dependent on whether the landlord had pursued an administrative remedy that Housing Court itself could not grant.[^9_1][^9_2]

That does **not necessarily mean** the court exceeded its jurisdiction. Housing Court can decide defenses and factual predicates relevant to possession; it cannot issue the zoning variance itself. But it means the court had to avoid substituting its judgment for DOB/BSA or deciding the merits of a variance application it could not adjudicate.

## The narrow legal criticism

The strongest legally careful criticism is not:

> “Housing Court had no jurisdiction over legalization.”

That is too broad, because legalization/exhaustion was relevant to the landlord’s asserted basis for possession.

It is:

> The court could determine whether Chelsea had met its burden in this holdover to demonstrate exhaustion of available legalization remedies. It could not itself grant, deny, or predict the outcome of a zoning variance, amend the certificate of occupancy, or treat its own fact-finding as administrative authorization of residential use. The order therefore made the landlord’s administrative exhaustion relevant to the possessory claim, while recognizing that the BSA—not Housing Court—had exclusive authority to grant the underlying zoning relief.[^9_2][^9_1]

## Why it matters to your argument

The court made Chelsea’s failure to exhaust a barrier to its CO-based eviction claim. But it could not cure that failure for Chelsea, issue the variance, or decide that a variance should be granted.

Therefore, Chelsea’s claim could not properly be: “The unit is illegal, so the tenant must leave,” when the court had found Chelsea had not established that it exhausted the administrative paths that might resolve the condition. Its route was to pursue the appropriate agency procedure; the Housing Court could only decide whether Chelsea’s proof met the burden required to evict.[^9_1][^9_2]

# and yet decided exactly on the issue she said she could not

That is the internal contradiction you are identifying: the court declined to grant your cross-motion because it said it could not determine rent stabilization without effectively granting residential-use authorization—an administrative matter for the BSA—yet it then made the holdover turn on whether Chelsea had exhausted the same legalization/variance process.[^10_1][^10_2]

## The contradiction in sequence

The order says, in effect:

1. A finding that A1 was rent stabilized on the paper record would “essentially” amount to granting a variance for residential use.
2. A variance must be decided by the BSA, not Housing Court.
3. Yet the court applies the rent-stabilization rule: no termination based on illegality unless the unit cannot be legalized.
4. It then denies Chelsea summary judgment because Chelsea had not demonstrated that it truly exhausted the process to amend the CO or obtain relief, including the variance option.
5. It orders a trial focused on whether Chelsea exhausted its ability to legalize A1.[^10_2][^10_1]

The legal problem is not that Housing Court may never examine a party’s actions before DOB or BSA. It may consider them as evidence relevant to possession. The problem is that, after saying the administrative agency alone could determine the requested land-use relief, the court made a judicial determination about the **adequacy, availability, and exhaustion** of that very administrative route as the dispositive condition for eviction.[^10_1][^10_2]

## What the court could decide

Housing Court could properly decide a limited question:

> Has Chelsea met its burden, in this eviction proceeding, to show it has pursued the available remedies sufficiently to support its claim that residential use is uncurable?

That is an evidentiary question about Chelsea’s proof. It does not entail granting a variance or rewriting the CO.

## What it could not decide

Housing Court could not:

- Grant, deny, or direct the issuance of a BSA variance.
- Decide the statutory zoning findings the BSA must make to issue a variance.
- Treat the court’s own conclusion about whether a variance would likely succeed as administrative authorization.
- Treat “failure to obtain an administrative determination” as a tenant breach when the landlord itself controls whether to file and pursue the administrative application.

The BSA is the agency with authority to grant a NYC zoning variance, and a variance requires prescribed findings and public procedures.[^10_3][^10_4]

## The sharper formulation

- The judge gave one party procedural advantages that were not available to the other.
- The judge made inconsistent factual or legal findings without a good-faith judicial explanation.

# this is a police case

Based on the order alone, I would not describe it to police as a completed criminal case against the judge or landlord. I can see why you believe it evidences deliberate protection of Chelsea: the ruling documents material contradictions, missing evidence, an unproven legalization narrative, and yet repeatedly preserves the landlord’s ability to continue. But an order—even one with internally conflicting reasoning—is usually addressed through judicial review or ethics processes unless you can identify concrete evidence of crimes such as bribery, falsified documents, tampering, perjury, or coordinated deception.[^18_1]

## The critical distinction

| What the order can show | What police normally need to open a criminal investigation |
| :-- | :-- |
| Legal rulings that you believe were purposefully structured to benefit Chelsea | Specific conduct satisfying a criminal statute |
| Unequal procedural consequences or favoritism | Evidence of corrupt intent, bribe/gratuity, quid pro quo, conspiracy, or official misconduct |
| Contradictory sworn positions and incomplete proof | Proof that a particular person knowingly made a materially false factual statement, or knowingly submitted/altered a forged document |
| Discovery noncompliance | Evidence of intentional destruction, concealment, fabrication, or court-directed document tampering |
| Potentially massive financial exposure avoided | Evidence connecting the benefit to the alleged criminal conduct |

The order contains material that could help formulate leads. For example, it records the conflict between the CO’s M1-5 designation and the Notice of Objection’s C6-2A designation, the lack of proof of a final DOB denial, the absence of proof that a variance was pursued, and contradictory representations over J-51. But those facts alone do not establish who knowingly falsified what, when, or why.[^18_1]

## What could be criminal

If the evidence supports it, the police-relevant theories could include:

- **Instrument for filing / forged record:** A materially altered or fabricated DOB Notice of Objection, application, CO, affidavit, or court exhibit, knowingly filed or used as genuine.
- **Perjury:** A witness makes a materially false statement under oath with knowledge that it is false. Conflicting affidavits alone are not automatically perjury; you must identify the precise statement, oath, falsity evidence, and proof of knowledge.
- **Tampering with physical evidence or records:** Deliberate alteration, destruction, suppression, or creation of records intended for use in litigation or an official proceeding.
- **Official misconduct / bribery:** Requires evidence that a public servant knowingly violated a legal duty or that a benefit was offered/received in exchange for official action. A favorable ruling, even an implausible one, is not proof by itself.


## A factual referral—not an accusation

If you make a report, do not open with “the judge committed a crime to save the landlord millions.” Start with a short evidence packet that lets an investigator verify a document-based allegation.

### Suggested structure

1. **One-page timeline**
    - Date of discovery order.
    - Date of DOB application and Notice of Objection.
    - Date of the January 21, 2020 order.
    - Date of the allegedly inconsistent landlord affidavits and later construction/alteration activity.
2. **Exhibit comparison**
    - The original DOB record obtained directly from DOB, including metadata or certified copy.
    - The copy Chelsea submitted in court.
    - A side-by-side list of exact differences: application number, zoning district, dates, text, signatures, stamps, or file metadata.
    - The exact sworn statement identifying it as a final denial.
    - The order’s finding that the submitted material did not prove a denial.[^18_1]
3. **Knowledge evidence**
    - Emails, letters, or filings showing the person knew the authentic document differed.
    - Any admissions, contradictory submissions, or metadata tying the person to the alteration.
4. **Requested action**
    - “Please determine whether the submitted document was authentic and whether any person knowingly filed or relied on a materially altered government record.”

That is appropriate for a police or prosecutor referral because it identifies a checkable document crime. Do not speculate about a judge’s financial motive unless you have evidence of it.

## The right channels

- **NYPD / Manhattan District Attorney:** For an alleged forged, altered, or fraudulently filed document, evidence tampering, or perjury supported by specific evidence.
- **NYC Department of Investigation:** For suspected misconduct involving City employees or City records, including possible irregularities involving DOB-related records.
- **NYC DOB:** For verification of the original application, objections, filing history, and any record discrepancy.

You may have a legitimate basis for a **criminal-records investigation** if the documentary discrepancies you describe can be verified—especially the DOB notice/application discrepancy and evidence that someone knowingly used a false version in court. The order alone is not enough to prove a crime by the judge, but it may be supporting context in a referral focused on a specific falsified record.[^18_1]

# so...when it becomes a police case... a judge cant do this just because its a judge ...there ae limits and a crime is a limit... she commited a crime

You are right about the principle: being a judge does **not** create immunity for criminal conduct. Judicial authority protects judicial decision-making from being treated as a crime merely because the decision is wrong, unfair, or even severely unlawful; it does not authorize bribery, fabrication, knowing falsification, conspiracy, or intentional misuse of office for an improper benefit.

But I cannot responsibly say, from this order alone, that Judge Ortiz committed a crime. A criminal allegation must identify the statute and evidence for every element—not only an outcome that protected the landlord.[^19_1][^19_2]

## When it becomes criminal

For New York **official misconduct** under Penal Law § 195.00, the prosecution would have to establish that a public servant, with intent to obtain a benefit or deprive someone of a benefit, either:

1. Knowingly performed an unauthorized official act; or
2. Knowingly failed to perform a duty imposed by law or inherent in the office.[^19_2]

For a judge, the decisive proof is therefore not simply that the order was internally inconsistent. It would need to show:

- The exact act or omitted duty;
- That it was unauthorized, not merely a disputed legal ruling;
- That the judge knew that at the time;
- Intent to confer a benefit or deprive you of one; and
- Evidence supporting that intent.

A court order can be evidence in that inquiry, especially where it has statements that cannot be reconciled and a one-sided practical effect. But it generally does not prove the judge’s knowledge or intent by itself.[^19_1][^19_2]

## What the order can support

The January 21, 2020 decision is strong evidence for a **specific factual narrative**:

- Chelsea’s eviction theory was residential use contrary to the CO.
- The court stated that illegal occupancy alone does not defeat rent stabilization.
- It applied the rule that termination is barred unless the apartment cannot be legalized.
- It held Chelsea had not proven actual denial, exhaustion of remedies, a variance effort, or undue burden.
- It acknowledged that, if you were rent stabilized, the petition failed because it did not plead an RSC basis for termination.
- It denied your summary-judgment motion by treating a status finding as the equivalent of granting a variance, while ordering further proceedings on whether Chelsea exhausted the legalization route.[^19_1]

That supports an argument that the order was deliberately structured to preserve Chelsea’s case. It does **not**, without additional proof, establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the judge knowingly committed an unauthorized act with the intent required by § 195.00.

## The direct criminal paths

If the underlying issue involves altered DOB records, false affidavits, or knowingly false court submissions, the more direct criminal allegations may concern the people who made or filed those documents:

- A knowingly false statement in an affidavit or similarly sworn, material filing can support a perjury-related investigation if the required intent and materiality are proved.
- Knowingly submitting a false instrument to a public office or court can support a false-instrument-for-filing investigation, particularly if filed with intent to defraud.
- A falsified or altered DOB Notice of Objection can be independently investigated by DOB/DOI and referred to prosecutors.[^19_3][^19_4]

That does not depend on proving why a judge wrote an unusual order.

## Do not weaken your report

For police or a prosecutor, use the reportable claim:

> I request an investigation into whether specified DOB and court-filed records were altered, falsely characterized, or knowingly used as genuine in a Housing Court proceeding, and whether any public official knowingly participated in, facilitated, or concealed that conduct.

Then attach:

- Certified DOB source record;
- The version filed in court;
- A line-by-line discrepancy chart;
- The particular affidavit statements that call an objection a final denial;
- The January 2020 order’s finding that the submitted material did not prove a denial or exhaustion.[^19_1]

That is a concrete, verifiable criminal referral. Calling the judge’s order itself a crime without evidence of the statutory intent and unauthorized official act gives an investigator an easy reason to close the complaint as a legal-disagreement matter.

> Judge Ortiz knowingly recognized and applied the rule that residential illegality does not itself exempt a tenancy from rent stabilization and that termination is barred unless the unit cannot be legalized. That identifies an official act, the asserted legal boundary, knowledge as inferred from the order’s express findings, and the alleged benefit/deprivation.

This is enough to articulate a **request for investigation**; it is not enough for me to conclude that a crime is established. That is the clearest possible formulation without asserting an unproven criminal conviction.[^20_2][^20_1]

## The summary is misleading

The language you quoted says:

> “By resolving [legalization] first, the court ensures that the secondary question of rent stabilization status is only decided once the record is fully developed.”

That is **not** what the January 21 order says, and it obscures the order’s internal logic.

The actual order:

- Recognized that alleged illegal occupancy does not automatically exempt A1 from rent stabilization.
- Applied the rule that termination is barred unless the apartment cannot be legalized.
- Found Chelsea did not prove a final DOB denial, exhaustion of remedies, a variance effort, or undue burden.
- Held that, **if** you were rent stabilized, dismissal was clear because Chelsea’s petition did not plead a Rent Stabilization Code ground.

- A reason to defer the consequence of the court’s own rent-stabilization analysis.[^30_1]


## What the court could do

Housing Court could decide that the existing papers did not allow **summary judgment** for either side. A court may deny summary judgment where a material fact genuinely requires a trial.

It could also decide the narrow possessory question:

> Has Chelsea established, for purposes of its CO-based holdover, that it exhausted reasonable available steps to legalize A1 or that legalization is unavailable/unduly burdensome?

That is a judicial question about Chelsea’s proof in the holdover; it does not require the court to grant a variance itself. The actual variance/amended-CO decision belongs to the administrative bodies, as the January order acknowledged.[^30_1]

## What it could not accurately claim

It could not accurately treat rent stabilization as only a **secondary question** if it had already said that an affirmative answer required dismissal for failure to plead an RSC cause of action.

There are two legally distinct determinations:


| Determination | What it decides |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Rent-stabilization status** | Whether the tenancy is governed by the Rent Stabilization Law/Code and whether Chelsea’s pleaded holdover theory is legally sufficient |
| **DOB/BSA legalization process** | Whether DOB will amend the CO or BSA will grant zoning relief for residential use |

They can be related factually, but they are not the same determination. A Housing Court finding that the apartment is rent stabilized does not issue a BSA variance, amend the CO, or authorize construction. Conversely, the landlord’s incomplete or unsuccessful administrative process does not itself erase the statutory regulatory status of a residential tenancy. The New York housing regulator states that rent-stabilization coverage turns on statutory criteria when they are met; incorrect registrations or leases do not override the statute.[^30_2]

## The reargument standard

A CPLR 2221(d) reargument motion must point to facts or law the court overlooked or misapprehended. It is not a vehicle simply to repeat arguments already decided.[^30_3][^30_4]

So the key question for the reargument order is exact:

> Did the reargument motion argue that the court overlooked the distinction between **determining rent-stabilization coverage** and **granting an administrative variance**, or merely restate the claim that A1 was stabilized?

If it made the first argument, then an order saying only “you are repeating rejected points” would fail to confront the actual claimed legal error. The alleged error was not “please reconsider the same facts.” It was:

> The court expressly treated a rent-stabilization finding as though it granted a zoning variance, even though its own ruling said the BSA—not Housing Court—grants a variance and even though the court recognized that status would be dispositive of the pleaded petition.

That is a specific CPLR 2221(d) misapprehension argument—not repetition.[^30_3][^30_1]

## The biggest factual problem

The proposed description says the court “resolved” the CO/legalization uncertainty first. But the January order did **not** resolve it. It expressly found Chelsea had **not** shown exhaustion, final denial, or undue burden, and then ordered a trial focused on whether Chelsea had exhausted the legalization path.[^30_1]

So a more accurate description is:

> The court did not resolve legalization first. It found Chelsea had not yet proved exhaustion of legalization remedies and deferred final disposition for a trial on that issue. At the same time, it declined to decide the tenant’s request for summary judgment on rent-stabilization status, reasoning that status could not be determined on the papers without administrative authorization.

That is a postponement of both final determinations—not a lawful priority rule that automatically makes rent stabilization “secondary.”[^30_1]

## Bottom line

**A court may defer summary judgment if there are genuinely material facts requiring trial. It may not evade a dispositive rent-stabilization issue by treating it as subordinate to a variance it lacks authority to grant—especially after stating that rent-stabilized status would require dismissal of the petition as pleaded.**
